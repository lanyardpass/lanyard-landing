I knew Lanyard needed a weather system. You're deciding whether today is a park day, and the sky has a vote.

Did it need one that accounts for wind speed and direction, the precipitation rate in millimeters per hour, how high the cloud ceiling is hanging over the castle, and the actual current phase of the moon?

No. Nobody asked for that. I built it because I'm a giant nerd and it made me happy. I hope it makes you happy too.

## The window

Lanyard's home screen has a card for each of your parks, and from the beginning I wanted those cards to feel like a window, not a dashboard.

<PullQuote>
A dashboard tells you it's 91 degrees. A window shows you the afternoon stacking up behind the skyline.
</PullQuote>

You look at a dashboard and you get a number. You look at a window and you think, yeah, that's a Florida summer sky, I know exactly how this day goes. That second thing is what the card does now.

## What it actually does

A few of my favorite things about it, in escalating order of nobody-asked-for-this.

- No two days ever draw the same sky. And no two parks share clouds, even when they're in the same storm system. Universal and SeaWorld are under the same weather, but not under the same cloud, because that's how being a few miles apart works.
- The cloud ceiling hangs low on humid days, because that's what humid days do.
- Lightning strikes more often when the storm is actually severe, and the whole scene flashes with it.
- On a day when storms are coming, you can watch the cumulus build toward the afternoon.
- When a hurricane warning posts, your card closes the parks before you've checked the news.
- The moon on your card is tonight's actual moon. This one isn't even weather. It stays in the list.

## The bugs are where it earned it

I'll spare you the shader math and tell you about the bugs instead, because a system like this earns its keep in the debugging.

For one glorious afternoon, the rain fell up. It would fall, slow down, and then drift back into the clouds like the sky was taking it back. It turns out that if you do the gust math slightly wrong, easing the wind literally rewinds the rain.

<NerdBox title="Why easing wind rewinds rain">
Each raindrop's position was computed as speed times time. Both of those change every frame, and that's the trap. When a gust eased off, the speed in that multiplication dropped, so the product dropped, so every drop's computed position moved backward along its own path. Up, in other words.

The fix is the one physics already knew about. Position isn't speed times time, it's the accumulation of speed over time. Integrate the speed into a running phase each frame and drops can slow down without un-falling. The broader lesson got written on the shop wall. Any time something's speed varies, animate from the accumulated distance, never from the instantaneous rate.
</NerdBox>

Then there was the haze. On overcast days, the whole gray deck would occasionally slide across the card like a wipe transition in a 1990s home video. I went through three theories. First the wind data, so I set wind to zero, and the slide kept sliding. Then the cloud drift math, and I actually found and fixed two real defects in it, except neither of them was this bug. The clue that cracked it was embarrassing in hindsight. Swipe the card carousel from the left, and the haze slid in from the right. The bug tracked my thumb, not the weather. The fade-in animation was accidentally grabbing the card's settle motion and replaying it on the weather layer. Three theories about meteorology, and the answer was the swipe.

The clouds themselves went through a phase where every one of them looked like a bunch of perfectly round balls glued together. Real cumulus is lumpy in an irregular, cauliflower way, and getting from "soap bubbles" to "cloud" took three separate passes and a rule where the little bumps only grow on a cloud's top, because the flat bottom is the whole point of a cumulus.

And then my favorite bug report of the whole project, which wasn't even filed as a bug.

> A beta tester watched a raindrop run down the glass, trailing a short wet track behind it, and described it with one word, "biological."

She was right. The trail is three times longer now and wobbles less. It reads as rain.

One more, for completeness. The clouds on two neighboring park cards were briefly identical, same shapes, same spots. Nobody would consciously notice. Everybody would feel it. Each card seeds its own sky now.

## What it won't do

Here's the line we drew, and it's load-bearing. Lanyard is an advisor with a window, not a weather app.

It won't tell you it'll rain at 7:14. It'll tell you afternoon storms are likely, and the sky on your card will already be building. If you're standing in the park deciding whether to duck into a show, you have a sky above you and a weather app in your pocket, and both of them beat me. What Lanyard answers is the passholder question, which is softer and honestly more useful. What's it like at my parks right now, and what kind of day is it going to be if I go?

That's the whole feature. It costs you nothing, it runs on knowledge the app already had, and it makes checking your parks feel less like reading a spreadsheet and more like looking out the window at a place you love.

The moon thing still makes me smile every time.
